OKCA Contrast Playground

OKCA 3.0 had to answer one question: should light text on a dark background score at least as high as the same colours reversed?

Reading studies of older adults find dark text on light reads better. The optics of an eye that scatters light, as with cataract, favour the reverse. OKCA 3.0.0 implements both. Its default follows the reading studies. An experimental scatter observer follows the optics.

How to read the scores

WCAG 2.x is shown unrounded, and its verdict compares the exact ratio to 4.5 and 7.0.

This reader is the OKCA 3.0 score with the 80-year-old eye's scatter, for either reader. Its verdict compares it with the threshold WCAG wrote for that reader: 4.5 for the 20/40 reader, 7.0 for the 20/80 reader.

OKCA never scores a pair above WCAG, so a pair that is enough for OKCA is enough for WCAG.

Verdicts on WCAG and the scorer table: passes AAA at 7.0 or more, passes AA at 4.5, large text only at 3.0, otherwise fails.

How the samples are drawn

Blur. Acuity is converted to CSS pixels through the CSS reference pixel, about 1.28 minutes of arc at arm's length. A 20/40 eye resolves detail of about 2 minutes, a 20/80 eye about 4, which gives a blur of about 1.6 and 3.1 px. It is approximate: the step from acuity to blur width is uncertain by up to a factor of two, and this page takes the stronger end.

Text size. For the low-vision reader the sample text is doubled. At arm's length, 16 px capitals are about 14 minutes of arc tall, and a 20/80 eye needs about 20 to read a letter. Acuity is measured with black letters on white, so 20/80 already describes that eye at close to the most contrast a screen can give; more contrast cannot carry it much past that size. A reader with that eye enlarges text, and so does this page. Doubling both the blur and the text leaves the letters as blurred, relative to their size, as for the 20/40 reader, so what changes the picture is contrast.

Veil. The text is shifted toward the background by the veil the chosen eye's scatter adds, exactly as the scatter score assumes. Both readers carry it: the 20/80 reader has everything the 20/40 reader has, and more. Its own scatter is not measured, so the 80-year-old veil is a floor for it, not an estimate.

Fairness. The blur and veil are the same on every pair, so only the colours change between samples.

Limits. A blur stands in for optical loss only; retinal loss such as macular degeneration is not modelled. Your screen, zoom, room and own eyes add their own effects. The scores, not the pictures, are the measure.

About these readers and eyes Readers

About 80 years old, 20/40. WCAG's 4.5:1 is written for this reader: 20/40 acuity carries a contrast-sensitivity loss of about 1.5, and 3 × 1.5 = 4.5. WCAG names age 80 only as the age at which 20/40 is typical.

Low vision, 20/80. WCAG's 7:1 is written for this reader, by the same reasoning. The two are points on one line, not two kinds of reader: required contrast grows with the loss. Past 20/80, WCAG expects readers to use assistive technology.

What WCAG does not model. Its reasoning is built from acuity alone. Cataract, glare and scatter are not mentioned; clouded eyes are only implicit in the people behind its data. The veils below are OKCA's addition, not part of WCAG's thresholds.

Scatter in the eye

None, the default observer is what OKCA 3.0 scores by default, and what the published scores assume.

80-year-old eye, full display is the glare veil of an 80-year-old eye, with the whole display around the text as its source. It applies to both readers, and is a floor for the 20/80 reader.

Dark mode and this model

The scatter veil grows with the background's brightness, so a dark background adds almost no veil and a light one adds a lot. For the 80-year-old eye, near-black (#121212) on white falls from 18.4 to 5.0 while white on near-black falls only from 15.6 to 15.3, and no dark-on-light pair reaches 7.0. Under this model, dark mode is the easiest way to pass, and for that eye the only way to AAA.

That is a claim about the score, not about reading. The reading studies still find dark text on light reads better for typical older readers; the reversal is measured in eyes with clouded media, such as cataract. The 80-year-old eye also assumes the whole display is bright behind the text, its worst case; a weaker veil returns much of the light-mode region. And light text on dark has costs the model does not score: halation for readers with astigmatism, and a wider pupil in a darker room.

The scatter observer is experimental and outside semver: it may change or be removed in any minor release. Use it as contrast(fg, bg, { observer: 'scatter' }).
